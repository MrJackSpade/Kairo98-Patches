# English style for anime-literate PC-98 VN readers

The reader knows common Japanese address terms, school relationships, food, pop culture, and holidays. Translate the story into fluent adult English while preserving the Japanese social and cultural information that this reader expects to see. This is the house style for model-generated candidates; it is not a substitute for review of the Japanese.

## Rules for the translation model

1. **Keep forms of address.** Retain spoken name suffixes such as `-san`, `-chan`, `-kun`, and `-sama`, and address terms such as `senpai`, `kohai`, and `sensei`. Keep the distinction between suffixes and standalone forms. Do not add an honorific that the source does not use or silently drop one when a change in address matters.
2. **Keep kinship-style vocatives.** If a character calls someone `Onee-san`, `Onee-chan`, `Onii-san`, or `Onii-chan`, retain that form rather than writing “Big Sister,” “Big Brother,” or an invented name. These can signal affection or social position without asserting literal kinship. When narration simply states a confirmed family relationship, natural English such as “my older sister” is appropriate.
3. **Keep Japanese names and nicknames.** Use one romanization and the established name order for each game. Preserve who calls whom by surname, given name, nickname, or title, and notice changes over time. Do not replace Japanese names with Western names.
4. **Keep familiar cultural referents.** Prefer `Obon`, `onsen`, `bento`, `onigiri`, `kimono`, `yukata`, `manga`, `doujin`, `otaku`, `yen`, and similar established terms when the Japanese referent itself matters. Preserve places, shrines, school clubs, and era-specific details. Do not turn Obon into a generic “summer festival” or yen into dollars. Translate generic words such as 神社 as “shrine” when no distinct term is needed.
5. **Translate ordinary language.** Audience familiarity is not a license to leave every Japanese word in romaji. Translate ordinary verbs, nouns, and connective speech into natural English. `Keikaku` normally becomes “plan”; keep it only when the word itself is a title, catchphrase, pun, or deliberate reference. Never add a “means plan” gloss to ordinary dialogue.
6. **Write for adults.** Preserve the speaker’s bluntness, politeness, humor, awkwardness, profanity, and sexual explicitness. Do not censor, soften, infantilize, or add cheerful dub-style catchphrases. Avoid modern American slang unless the source calls for it.
7. **Preserve facts before polish.** Do not invent a family relationship, motive, gender, cultural equivalent, or joke to make a line sound familiar. If an idiom can be rendered naturally without changing its point, do so. If the effect cannot survive without explanation, flag it for review outside the displayed `en` text.
8. **Keep the patch mechanics.** Preserve every runtime token exactly, including percent markers such as `%001`, brace markers such as `{X1}`, and format placeholders such as `%s`; repeated tokens must stay repeated. Preserve intentional line breaks and the source’s sequence of events. Do not put translator notes or explanations inside `en`.

## What the subtitle sample showed

I inspected 15 English subtitle tracks or sidecars from 13 series in the local Plex library. The embedded tracks were sampled for the first 10–15 minutes; the sidecars were read as supplied. The sample included *Azumanga Daioh*, *Bocchi the Rock!*, *Lucky Star*, *Nichijou* (two different tracks), *Oniichan wa Oshimai!*, *Yuru Yuri*, *Rascal Does Not Dream of Bunny Girl Senpai*, and *My Senpai Is Annoying*. Several independent tracks retained `-chan`; the last three also retained `senpai`, `-kun`, or `-san`. The *Oniichan wa Oshimai!* track kept `Onii-chan` as direct address while using English for a descriptive brother relationship. Other sampled tracks kept terms including `manga`, `doujin`, `ramen`, `kimono`, `yen`, and `daikichi`. These are observations about this library, not a claim that all fansubs follow one style.

Fansub groups themselves differ. [Anything Goes Fansubs](https://anythingooo.wordpress.com/faq/) says it keeps honorifics because removing them loses relationship information. The [#PocketMonsters style guide](https://pocketmonsters.net/content/subbingstyleguide) selectively keeps some honorifics and translates other roles. This project's audience calls for the more preservation-oriented choice above. A [Japanese dictionary entry for お姉さん](https://dictionary.goo.ne.jp/word/%E5%BE%A1%E5%A7%89%E3%81%95%E3%82%93/) confirms that it can address a young woman as well as a sister; [Japan's tourism agency](https://www.japan.travel/en/spot/99/) uses `Obon` as the festival's name.
